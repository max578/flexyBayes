One formula, three engines, a Bayesian inheritance

1The variance-structure tradition

Most statistics teaching stops at the mean: y ~ x. Yet in field trials, animal breeding, genetics and longitudinal medicine the decisive information lives in the variance — how much spread is between plots, between animals, between time-points, and how those sources correlate. The tradition that made this routine is reml (restricted, or residual, maximum likelihood), and its most influential software incarnation is ASReml, which gave practitioners a compact grammar for the linear mixed model

y= Xβ+ Zu+ e, u∼N(0,G), e∼N(0,R)

where Xβ is the fixed part (the population mean structure), Zu the random part (group-level deviations with covariance G), and e the residuals with covariance R. ASReml's wedge was a three-line declarative grammar —

fixed  = trait ~ treatment
random = ~ block + variety
rcov   = ~ ar1(row):ar1(col)     # a spatial residual structure

— letting you say, precisely, “estimate these means, treat these as exchangeable draws, and let the residuals follow that correlation in space or time.” That fixed / random / rcov split is exactly the syntax flexyBayes adopts.

The quiet power in the random part is partial pooling. A random effect does not estimate each group in isolation; it pulls noisy small-group estimates toward the overall mean, borrowing strength across groups (Figure 1). A group seen only a few times is told, in effect, “until you show me otherwise, you look like the population.”

2Where Bayesian statistics came from

The method now used to filter spam, decode genomes and rank search results began with a Presbyterian minister and an argument about miracles.

A minister and a posthumous essay

Thomas Bayes (c. 1701–1761) was an English Nonconformist — a Presbyterian minister, son of one of the first Nonconformist ministers ordained in England, who had studied logic and divinity at Edinburgh. He published almost nothing on mathematics in his lifetime. His one celebrated work, An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances, was found among his papers after his death and read to the Royal Society in 1763 — published the following year — by his friend Richard Price, who edited it heavily, supplied worked numerical examples and an appendix, and by some accounts contributed roughly half of the final paper.1

Bayes asked an inverse question. The probability calculus of his day — De Moivre's Doctrine of Chances — ran forwards: given a known chance, what outcomes follow? Bayes turned it around. Given the outcomes we have actually seen, what can we say about the unknown chance that produced them? That is the question every scientist faces, and the answer is the rule that bears his name.

The probability of any event is the ratio between the value at which an expectation depending on the happening of the event ought to be computed, and the value of the thing expected upon its happening. — Thomas Bayes, opening definition of the 1763 Essay
The “God” in the origin story. Price was not only a mathematician but a moral philosopher and minister, and he read Bayes's result through the lens of natural theology: inverse probability as a way of reasoning from the observed order of the world back toward its cause. He turned it, pointedly, against David Hume, whose 1748 essay Of Miracles argued that no testimony could rationally establish a miracle, since a miracle is maximally improbable. Price's reply was probabilistic — Bayes's mathematics shows how the accumulation of even fallible, independent testimony can overwhelm a very small prior probability, so a sufficiently corroborated report of an improbable event can rationally raise belief.2 Whether Bayes himself intended a theological target is debated — his interest may simply have come from De Moivre — but his editor launched the idea into the world as, among other things, an instrument of natural theology: inverse probability as a way to argue from effects back to a first cause.3

The method was carried much further by Pierre-Simon Laplace, who rediscovered and generalised it from the 1770s, called it the “probability of causes,” and applied it to real problems — the masses of the planets, the ratio of male to female births. For a century it was known as inverse probability; the adjective Bayesian became common only in the twentieth century, during a revival led by Jeffreys, de Finetti and Savage, and made practical for hard problems by the arrival of Markov-chain Monte Carlo in the 1990s — the engines (Stan, greta, INLA) that flexyBayes drives today.

3The ideas you actually need

Bayesian inference is one equation and a change of attitude. The attitude: a parameter is not a fixed unknown to be point-estimated but a quantity we hold beliefs about, expressed as a probability distribution and updated by data.

Bayes' theorem — the update rule

p(θ∣D) = p(D∣θ) p(θ) p(D)

The denominator p(D) is just the constant that makes the right-hand side a proper distribution, so the working form is a proportionality:

p(θ∣D) ⏟posterior ∝ p(D∣θ) ⏟likelihood × p(θ) ⏟prior
  • Prior — what we believe about the parameter before this data set (from theory, past studies, or a deliberately weak “let the data speak” choice).
  • Likelihood — how well each candidate value explains the data; the same object frequentist maximum likelihood maximises.
  • Posterior — the updated belief: the prior reshaped by the evidence. Because it is a full distribution, every summary (mean, interval) falls out of it directly (Figure 2).

A few consequences are worth keeping:

ConceptWhat it gives you
Credible interval“there is a 95% probability the parameter lies in [a, b]” — the statement people think a confidence interval makes, here literally true.
Conjugacyspecial prior–likelihood pairs whose posterior is available in closed form. A coin with a Beta(a,b) prior, after y heads in n tosses, has posterior Beta(a+y,b+n−y) — the analytic anchor a validation can check against.
MCMC / HMCwhen no closed form exists, draw samples from the posterior by simulating a Markov chain (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo in Stan and greta); the draws are the answer.
Shrinkagea random effect is a hierarchical prior; the posterior pulls small-group estimates toward the mean — the mixed model of §1 and the Bayesian model are the same object.
The bridge. random = ~Subject says “subjects are exchangeable draws from a population” — which is a prior on the subject effects. flexyBayes makes that identity operational: it takes the ASReml-style formula and hands it to a Bayesian engine.

4flexyBayes, illustrated

Here is the canonical sleep-deprivation study (sleepstudy: reaction time against days of restricted sleep, 18 subjects, 180 observations). One formula, written once:

library(flexyBayes)
fit <- flexybayes(
  fixed   = Reaction ~ Days,   # population slope of reaction time on days
  random  = ~Subject,          # each subject keeps their own intercept
  data    = sleepstudy,
  backend = "inla"             # or "greta" (TensorFlow HMC), or "brms" (Stan HMC)
)
coef(fit)                      # canonical-named posterior means

The same three lines, with backend = swapped, run the same formula through three different estimation engines. The validation study fits all three on this exact model and checks them against lme4 (REML) and, where the engine is deterministic, against a hand-written backend fit. Two things are worth seeing. First, the INLA route is numerically identical to a hand-written INLA::inla() fit (difference exactly zero) — direct evidence that the formula-to-backend translation is faithful, not approximate. Second, three different samplers and the frequentist estimator agree to well within their uncertainty — agreement across paradigms, the strongest kind of corroboration a number can carry.

A convergence caveat. The greta run above used a modest warm-up and flexyBayes reported that the sampler had not fully converged (R-hat up to 2.66). The estimate still fell within tolerance; a publication fit would raise the warm-up. The convergence diagnostic is reported alongside the estimate.

5To its heights

The point of one syntax over three engines is that you can choose the engine by the problem and cross-check across them.

Triangulation

Because the same model can be fit three ways, agreement between the engines becomes a built-in robustness check. flexyBayes exposes this through its triangulate() method: fit in INLA (fast, deterministic) and in Stan (full MCMC), and if the posteriors agree you have strong evidence the answer is real, not an artefact of one engine's approximation. Figure 3 is triangulation in miniature.

Scale

The three engines have different sweet-spots, and one syntax lets you meet a dataset where it is:

EngineStrengthReach for it when…
INLAfast, deterministic approximate inference; no samplinglarge spatial / temporal and structured-covariance models where MCMC would be hopeless
gretaTensorFlow back-end; GPU-capable HMClarge, custom, differentiable models that benefit from hardware acceleration
brms / Stangold-standard HMC; rich family and prior supportcomplex likelihoods where you want the most trusted sampler

On top of the engines, flexyBayes carries an indexing representation of the design built to scale, plus higher-level entry points for genomics-scale work — among its exported verbs are fb_gwas() (genome-wide association), fb_gblup_cv() (genomic best-linear-unbiased prediction with cross-validation), structured-covariance constructors, and aggregation paths for grouped data. The same fixed / random formula that fits 18 subjects of sleep data is the formula that fits thousands of genotypes.

Full circle. Price used inverse probability to argue that enough independent evidence can overturn a strong prior. Two and a half centuries later that is exactly what a triangulated, large-scale flexyBayes fit does: it accumulates evidence across engines and across thousands of units until the posterior is sharp enough to act on. The minister's instrument, industrialised.
